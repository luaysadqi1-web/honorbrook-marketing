Can I roll over my TSP to an IRA or 401(k)? What federal employees should know

Yes. Once you leave federal service, the Thrift Savings Plan lets you move all or part of your balance to a traditional IRA or an eligible employer plan, or you can simply leave it in the TSP. Choosing well comes down to fees, investment choices, income needs and taxes.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

If you have spent years in federal service or the military, your Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) may be your largest retirement account. When you separate or retire, the same questions come up: should I move it, where, and what does it cost me? This guide covers the choices in plain English. It is general information; the TSP's own rules at tsp.gov always control.

The short version
  • You can keep your money in the TSP if your vested balance is $200 or more, and leave it as long as you want, per tsp.gov.
  • As a separated participant you can take a partial or total withdrawal, set up installments, or buy an annuity, per tsp.gov.
  • Many payments can be rolled over to an IRA or eligible employer plan. Required minimum distributions cannot.

Your main choices after you separate

  • Leave it in the TSP. You keep the plan's structure and can still move money between its funds. Nothing forces a decision.
  • Roll it to a traditional IRA. This gives you a wider menu of investments and withdrawal choices, but the account is no longer in the TSP and you will pay whatever fees the IRA provider charges.
  • Roll it to a new employer plan. If you take a job whose plan accepts rollovers, this can keep everything in one place.
  • Take installments or a partial withdrawal. Money you take out is generally taxable income in the year you receive it, unless it is Roth money that meets the rules.
  • Buy an annuity. The TSP offers an annuity option, and an annuity from an insurance company is another route. See the section below.

Direct vs. indirect rollovers: the 60-day rule

A direct rollover sends the money straight from the TSP to the new account. An indirect rollover pays you first, and you generally have 60 days to deposit it. According to TSP materials, the TSP withholds taxes on an indirect payment: on a $10,000 distribution, $2,000 is withheld and you receive $8,000, so rolling over the full $10,000 means covering the $2,000 yourself. A direct rollover avoids that problem, which is why most people choose it.

Traditional vs. Roth money

Your TSP may hold traditional (pre-tax) and Roth balances. The TSP accepts rollovers from traditional IRAs and eligible plans into the traditional balance, and Roth 401(k), 403(b) and 457(b) money into the Roth balance, but it does not accept money from a Roth IRA, per tsp.gov. Moving between traditional and Roth can create a tax bill, so talk to a tax professional first.

Required minimum distributions

Under current IRS rules, required minimum distributions begin at age 73 for people born from 1951 through 1959, and at 75 for those born in 1960 or later. An RMD cannot be rolled over, and missing one can trigger an excise tax. Ask the TSP or your tax professional how your own deadline works.

Where an annuity can (and can't) fit

Some retirees want part of their savings to produce income they cannot outlive. An annuity is one way to do that, and so is the TSP's own annuity. Before moving money, compare them carefully:

  • Surrender charges. Many annuities charge a fee if you withdraw early. Ask for the schedule in writing.
  • Fees and limits. Riders, caps and participation rates can reduce what you receive. Ask for every cost.
  • Guarantees. They are backed by the issuing insurance company's claims-paying ability, not by the government.
  • Liquidity. Money you move out of the TSP may be harder to reach later.

An annuity is not an investment in the stock market, and it is not right for everyone. See our guides on IRA rollovers into annuities and fixed indexed annuities, and our annuities page.

Questions to ask before you move TSP money

  • What are all the annual fees in the new account, compared with the TSP?
  • Will I lose any feature I value, such as the TSP's fund menu?
  • Is this a direct rollover, and who completes the transfer paperwork?
  • How will this affect my taxes this year and in future years?
  • If an annuity is involved, what are the surrender charges, fees, and who stands behind the guarantee?

Common questions

TSP Rollover FAQ

Can I roll my TSP into an IRA while I am still working for the government?
The TSP lists rollovers out as an option for separated participants, so in general you need to have left federal service first. Rules for in-service withdrawals are narrower and have their own conditions, so confirm on tsp.gov before planning around one.
Do I have to move my TSP when I retire?
No. Per tsp.gov you can keep your account in the TSP after you separate if your vested balance is $200 or more, and you can leave it there as long as you want to. Moving it is a choice, not a requirement.
Is it a good idea to move TSP money into an annuity?
It depends on the person. An annuity can add an income option, but it can also carry surrender charges, fees and limits that the TSP does not, and guarantees depend on the issuing company's claims-paying ability. Compare it with the TSP's own options before deciding.
