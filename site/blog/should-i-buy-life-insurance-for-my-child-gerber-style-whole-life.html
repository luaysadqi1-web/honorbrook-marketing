Should I Buy Life Insurance for My Child? Pros, Cons and the Gerber-Style Whole Life Option

It can be a reasonable long-term move for some families, but it should not come before protecting the parents.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

When a baby arrives, so do offers for children's life insurance, including whole life policies with names that sound like savings plans. Is it a smart move or a distraction? The answer depends on your family's priorities. This guide lays out the pros and cons so you can decide with a clear head.

Important: this article is educational, not personalized advice. Annuities and life insurance guarantees depend on the issuing company's claims-paying ability, can carry surrender charges and fees, and are not bank products or investments. Speak with a licensed professional and your tax advisor before moving money.

First, protect the people who earn the income

Life insurance replaces income that others rely on. Children typically do not earn income and no one depends on theirs. That is why most advisors recommend covering parents first, ideally with affordable term life that is large enough to support the family if a parent dies. See how much life insurance you need. If the parents are not adequately covered, a child's policy is generally lower priority.

What a child's policy can do

A juvenile life policy, often permanent whole life, can provide:

  • A small death benefit. It can help cover final expenses in the tragic event of a child's death, though this is rarely the main reason to buy.
  • Locked-in insurability. The child's health at the time of purchase is locked in. If they develop a condition later, the policy remains.
  • Guaranteed cash value growth, typically modest, and level premiums.
  • Options to convert or increase. Many policies offer riders that let the child buy more coverage later without new medical evidence. See guaranteed insurability riders.
  • Permanent coverage into adulthood that can last the child's lifetime.

The Gerber-style whole life option

Plans marketed for children, sometimes called "grow-up" or juvenile whole life, are small whole life policies where the coverage amount may grow at certain ages. We discuss these generally and not as a recommendation of any one brand. If you are comparing, ask for the actual cash value table and the cost per year, not just the headline benefit.

The cons to weigh

  1. Low need for the death benefit. The child has no dependents.
  2. Slow cash value growth. Early years often show little cash value after costs.
  3. Opportunity cost. The same money might do more in a college savings plan or retirement account. See life insurance vs. 529.
  4. Commitment. Premiums are paid for many years, and dropping the policy early can mean losing some value.
  5. Pressure marketing. Some pitches overstate the benefits.
A fair test: If you would not buy the policy without the savings feature, compare the cash value schedule against other ways to save for the same goal.

Who may find it worthwhile

  • Families with a history of conditions that could make future insurance harder to obtain.
  • Parents who want to start a permanent policy early at a low premium and are comfortable with the long-term commitment.
  • Grandparents who want to gift a lasting asset.
  • Families who have already secured their own coverage and funded other goals.

Who may want to skip it

  • Families whose parents lack adequate life insurance.
  • Households without an emergency fund or retirement savings.
  • Parents who mainly want college savings, where other vehicles may be more flexible.
  • Anyone unsure they can keep paying premiums.

Questions to ask before buying

  • What is the guaranteed cash value in years 5, 10, and 20?
  • Does the policy include a guaranteed insurability or conversion option, and what does it cost?
  • What happens to ownership and control when the child becomes an adult?
  • Are premiums guaranteed level?
  • Who owns the policy, and who is the beneficiary?
  • How does the cost compare to other savings options?

How to think about it

A child's policy is a long-term, modest tool. It may complement, but should not replace, adequate coverage for parents and a sound savings plan. Read our guide on how cash value life insurance for kids works.

How to decide in a single sitting

Sit down with your partner and ask four questions. Are both parents covered adequately? Are emergency savings and retirement contributions on track? Is there a family health history that makes future insurability a worry? Can we afford the premium for many years without strain? If the answers are yes, no, yes, yes, a child's policy may be a reasonable addition. If the first two answers are no, it can wait. This simple filter keeps the decision grounded in your actual situation instead of a sales presentation.

Transferring ownership later

Many juvenile policies are owned by a parent during childhood and can be transferred to the child as an adult. Ask how and when ownership transfers, whether premiums change, and what taxes or paperwork are involved. Knowing the plan for adulthood helps you see whether the policy will still make sense then.

Common questions

Child Life Insurance FAQ

Do children need life insurance?
Children do not usually have income to replace, so most families prioritize coverage for the parents first. Child policies are mainly about locking in insurability, building small cash value, and covering final expenses.
What is juvenile whole life insurance?
It is a whole life policy on a child that builds cash value over time, has level premiums, and can often continue into adulthood, sometimes with options to increase coverage.
Is a child's policy a good way to save?
Cash value grows slowly and there are costs, so it may not be the most efficient savings tool. It can be a supplement for families who value the guarantees and permanence.
