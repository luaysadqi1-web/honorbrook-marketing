Do I Need Disability Insurance? What It Covers and Who Needs It

Disability insurance replaces part of your paycheck if an illness or injury keeps you from working. If your income pays your bills and you lack savings or employer coverage to last for months, it deserves a look. Here is how it works and what to compare.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

Most of us insure the house and the car, yet the thing that pays for both is our ability to earn a paycheck. Disability insurance is built to protect that. It does not cover medical bills, which health insurance handles. It replaces part of your income while you cannot work.

The risk is real. A Social Security Administration fact sheet states that about 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age. Check the fact sheet for its current wording and date.

What disability insurance does

If an illness or injury keeps you from working, a disability policy pays a monthly benefit, usually a percentage of your income rather than all of it. You can use it for anything: rent or mortgage, groceries, utilities, or the premium on your health coverage. Policies differ widely in when payments start, how long they last, and what counts as being unable to work.

Who should look at it

  • The main earner. If a household runs on one paycheck, losing it hits everyone.
  • Self-employed people and small business owners. There is usually no employer plan behind you and no paid sick leave.
  • Anyone with limited savings. Ask how many months of bills you could cover with no income.
  • People whose work coverage is thin. Group coverage through an employer may be limited, may not follow you if you change jobs, and may pay a benefit that a policy of your own could supplement.

Why Social Security is not a safety net for most people

Social Security Disability Insurance exists, but it has a strict definition of disability and a waiting period. The Social Security Administration explains on its disability benefits page how it decides eligibility and when benefits begin. Many people who have a serious but temporary illness do not qualify, so it should not be your only plan.

Short-term vs. long-term

  • Short-term disability covers a limited period, often related to recovery from surgery, childbirth, or an injury.
  • Long-term disability picks up after the short-term period ends and can last for years or until a stated age, depending on the policy.

The terms that change what you actually get

  • Elimination period. The wait between becoming disabled and your first payment. A longer wait usually lowers the premium, but you must be able to cover that gap yourself.
  • Benefit period. How long payments continue once they start.
  • Definition of disability. Some policies pay if you cannot do your own occupation. Others only pay if you cannot do any occupation. This distinction matters a great deal at claim time.
  • Benefit amount. The share of your income the policy replaces.
  • Exclusions and renewability. Ask what the policy will not cover and whether the insurer can change your premium or cancel.

Group coverage vs. an individual policy

A plan through your employer is often inexpensive or free to you, which makes it a good start. Compare it with what you would need: what share of your pay it replaces, how it defines disability, and whether you keep it if you leave. An individual policy is yours to keep, and the insurer generally cannot cancel it because you changed jobs. Whether benefits are taxable can depend on who pays the premium, so ask a tax professional how it applies to you.

Common questions

Disability Insurance FAQ

Do I need disability insurance if I have health insurance?
Yes, they do different jobs. Health insurance pays medical bills. Disability insurance replaces part of your income when you cannot work, so you can keep paying rent, a mortgage and other living costs.
Does my employer's disability coverage mean I am covered?
Possibly in part. Employer plans often replace only a share of pay, can define disability narrowly and may end when you leave the job. Compare it with your monthly expenses before you rely on it.
What is an elimination period?
It is the waiting time between becoming disabled and your first benefit payment. Longer waits usually mean a lower premium, but you need savings to cover the gap.
