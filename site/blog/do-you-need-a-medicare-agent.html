Do you really need a Medicare agent? Here's the honest answer

You can enroll in Medicare entirely on your own — nobody's stopping you. So why do most people end up working with an agent anyway? Here's what an agent actually does, why it costs you nothing, and when skipping one is genuinely fine.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · August 2026

Medicare.gov lets anyone compare and enroll in a plan without ever speaking to another person. So the honest first question isn't "how do I find a good agent" — it's "do I need one at all?" The answer depends on what you're actually trying to solve, so let's separate what an agent does from what you can just as easily do yourself.

What a licensed agent actually does

  • Compares across carriers. An independent agent isn't limited to one company's plans — we pull options from 90+ carriers and lay them side by side.
  • Checks your doctors and drugs before you enroll. Provider networks and drug formularies change every year. We run your specific list against each plan instead of you finding out at the pharmacy counter.
  • Explains the tradeoffs in plain English. "Which plan is best" is rarely a simple answer — it depends on your health, budget, and how you use care.
  • Stays reachable after you enroll. Claims questions, a lost card, a plan that changes mid-year — a real phone number to call beats a hold queue.
What an agent is not: a replacement for a doctor, a guarantee of the "best" plan (there often isn't a single best plan — see Advantage vs. Medigap), or your only source of unbiased help. State Health Insurance Assistance Programs (SHIP) offer free, agent-free counseling too — they just can't enroll you in a specific plan.

Why it doesn't cost you anything

This is the part people find hardest to believe: your premium is set by Medicare and the carrier, not by whether you used an agent. Carriers pay agents a commission directly, completely separate from what you pay. Enroll yourself on Medicare.gov, call the carrier, or work with an independent agent — the premium is identical. The only thing that changes is how much comparison and support you get along the way.

Here's the part that makes it click for most people: carriers already build the cost of distribution — whether that's advertising, call centers, or agent commissions — into how they price and budget for every plan. An agent's commission isn't an extra fee layered on top of your premium; it's simply which distribution channel the carrier is paying for that particular enrollment. You don't save money by cutting out the agent, the same way you don't get a discount at a hotel by booking directly instead of through a travel site — the room rate was already going to be the room rate.

When going solo makes sense

If you already know exactly which plan you want, you've personally verified your doctors and medications are covered, and you're comfortable navigating enrollment sites directly — you genuinely don't need an agent. Plenty of people do this well, especially if their situation is simple and hasn't changed much year to year.

When an agent tends to save people the most trouble

  • You're comparing more than two or three plans and losing track of the differences
  • You take multiple prescriptions and want them checked against a formulary before you commit
  • You're not sure whether Medicare Advantage or a Medigap plan fits your situation better
  • Your health has changed recently and you're not sure how that affects your options
  • You just want someone to call when something goes wrong after enrollment

How to tell a good agent from a pushy one

A good independent agent will tell you when your current plan is still your best option — even though that means no new commission. A few signs worth watching for either way:

  • Good sign: they ask about your doctors and medications before recommending anything, not after.
  • Red flag: they only ever seem to recommend one carrier, no matter what you're looking for.
  • Good sign: they can explain in plain terms why a plan fits your specific situation, not just its general features.
  • Red flag: they create urgency to sign today, especially outside an actual enrollment deadline.
  • Good sign: they're upfront that they're paid by commission and explain how that works when you ask.

None of these signs alone means someone is dishonest — but if you notice more than one red flag, it's worth getting a second opinion before you enroll.

Common questions

Medicare Agents FAQ

Does it cost more to enroll through an agent?
No. Medicare and carriers set the same premium no matter how you enroll — directly, through Medicare.gov, or through a licensed agent. Carriers pay agents separately; it's never added to your premium.
Is a Medicare agent the same as a SHIP counselor?
No. SHIP counselors offer free, unbiased general guidance but can't recommend specific plans or enroll you. An agent can compare specific plans, check your doctors and drugs, and complete enrollment. Both are free; they serve different purposes.
When does it make sense to skip an agent?
If you already know which plan you want, have confirmed your doctors and medications are covered, and are comfortable enrolling directly, going solo works fine. An agent adds the most value when comparing multiple carriers or when you're unsure about coverage.
