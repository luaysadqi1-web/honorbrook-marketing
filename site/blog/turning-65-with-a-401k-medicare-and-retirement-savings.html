Turning 65 With a 401(k): What to Do About Medicare and Your Savings

Your 401(k) and Medicare decisions affect each other more than most people realize. Timing is everything.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

Turning 65 is a double milestone for many people. It is the age Medicare eligibility begins, and it is often when retirement planning gets serious. If you have a 401(k) or other retirement savings, your Medicare timing and your money decisions can affect each other. This guide covers the main connections in plain English. It is general information, not tax or financial advice.

Do you need to sign up for Medicare at 65?

It depends on whether you are still working and what coverage you have.

  • If you are retired or have no employer coverage, you should generally enroll during your Initial Enrollment Period, a seven-month window around your 65th birthday. Missing it can lead to lifetime late penalties for Part B and Part D.
  • If you still work for an employer with 20 or more employees and have group health coverage through that job, you may be able to delay Part B without a penalty and enroll later during a Special Enrollment Period.
  • If your employer is small or your coverage is not through current employment, different rules can apply. Check with your benefits office and Medicare before delaying.

Our Turning 65 guide walks through the enrollment windows.

The HSA catch

Many people with a high-deductible health plan contribute to a Health Savings Account (HSA). Once you enroll in any part of Medicare, you generally cannot contribute to an HSA. Part A can also begin up to six months back when you apply after 65, which can create an unexpected tax problem if you contributed during that period. If you plan to keep contributing, talk to your benefits administrator or tax professional about timing.

How retirement income affects Medicare premiums

Medicare Part B and Part D premiums can be higher for people with higher income. These income-related adjustments are based on your modified adjusted gross income from a tax return filed a couple of years earlier. Things that can increase it include:

  • Large withdrawals from traditional 401(k) or IRA accounts.
  • Roth conversions.
  • Selling a business or property with large gains.
  • Taking a lump-sum pension or rollover distribution that is taxable.
The takeaway: a big taxable withdrawal in one year can raise your Medicare premiums two years later. If your income is about to change, ask a tax professional how to time it.

What to do with the 401(k) when you retire

You generally have four options: leave it, roll it to a new employer plan, roll it to an IRA, or take the cash. Read what to do with your 401(k) when you retire or leave a job for the full list. At 65 there is one more consideration: required minimum distributions begin later in retirement, so how you structure withdrawals now affects your future taxes and Medicare premiums.

Where guaranteed income can fit

Some people build a base of income that does not depend on the market, using Social Security, a pension, and in some cases an annuity. Which mix fits depends on your spending needs, health, and how you feel about risk. We can explain how these products work, but a tax professional should weigh in before you move money.

A simple 65-year-old's checklist

  1. Confirm whether you are still covered by an employer plan and how it coordinates with Medicare.
  2. Mark your Initial Enrollment Period dates.
  3. Decide how to handle your HSA before enrolling.
  4. List your doctors and medications so you can compare plans.
  5. Estimate your retirement income and how it will be taxed.
  6. Review your life insurance and beneficiary designations.

Medicare plan choices

Once you enroll, you will choose between Original Medicare with a Medigap plan and a Medicare Advantage plan, plus drug coverage if needed. Our guide on Advantage vs. Medigap helps you think through the choice.

A few real-life scenarios

Someone working at a large company with employer coverage may keep that coverage and delay Part B, while someone who retires at 64 needs coverage in place by their Medicare start date. A person who sells a business at 66 may have one very high-income year that raises their Medicare premiums two years later. These examples are not advice, just reminders that timing and income both matter, and a short conversation before big decisions can prevent expensive surprises.

What to bring to a planning conversation

To make a short conversation productive, gather your most recent 401(k) statement, a rough monthly budget, your expected Social Security start date, a list of doctors and medications, and any employer benefit documents. With those in hand, it is much easier to line up Medicare timing, premium effects, and retirement income choices in the right order instead of making them one at a time.

Common questions

Turning 65 and 401(k) FAQ

Do I have to sign up for Medicare at 65 if I still work?
Not always. If you have coverage through a current employer, whether you can delay Part B without a penalty depends on the size of the employer and the type of coverage. Confirm with your benefits office before deciding.
Does my 401(k) income affect my Medicare premiums?
It can. Higher income can trigger income-related adjustments to Part B and Part D premiums, based on tax returns from a couple of years earlier.
Can I keep contributing to an HSA after 65?
Once you are enrolled in any part of Medicare, you generally cannot contribute to an HSA. Part A coverage can start retroactively, so timing needs care.
