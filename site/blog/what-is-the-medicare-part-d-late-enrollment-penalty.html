What is the Medicare Part D Late Enrollment Penalty, and can I avoid it?

The Part D late enrollment penalty is a permanent add-on to your monthly drug premium if you go 63 days or more in a row without Part D or other creditable drug coverage after you are first eligible. It equals 1% of the national base premium for each full uncovered month.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

Many people turn 65, feel healthy, and skip a drug plan. That can be a costly choice later. Medicare Open Enrollment runs October 15 – December 7, which makes this a good moment to check whether you owe a penalty or are about to start one. Here is how it works, in plain English.

The formula, per Medicare.gov
  • Penalty = 1% × the national base beneficiary premium × the number of full months you went without coverage.
  • The base premium is $38.99 in 2026 (Medicare.gov). CMS announced $41.33 for 2027 in its July 28, 2026 Part C & D announcement.
  • The result is rounded to the nearest $0.10 and added to your plan's monthly premium.

When does the penalty start?

According to Medicare.gov, a penalty may apply if, after your Initial Enrollment Period ends, you go 63 or more days in a row without Part D or other creditable prescription drug coverage. "Creditable" means the coverage is expected to pay, on average, at least as much as Medicare's standard drug coverage. Employer, union and some VA or TRICARE plans often qualify, and the plan should tell you each year. Keep those notices.

A simple example

Medicare.gov gives the model: if you waited 14 months after becoming eligible, the penalty is 14% of the base premium. At the 2026 base premium of $38.99, that works out to about $5.50 a month (14% × $38.99 ≈ $5.46, rounded to $5.50). The amount is recalculated as the base premium changes each year, and it generally stays for as long as you have Medicare drug coverage, even if you switch plans. This is an illustration of the formula, not a quote for your situation.

How to avoid it

  • Enroll in a Part D plan or a Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage during your Initial Enrollment Period, even if you take few prescriptions.
  • Keep creditable coverage if you stay on a job-based plan, and save the creditable-coverage notice.
  • Do not let a gap reach 63 days when you leave an employer plan. Enroll promptly.
  • Check for Extra Help. Medicare.gov says people who receive Extra Help do not pay the late enrollment penalty. Ask us or the Social Security Administration about eligibility.

Who gets caught by the penalty most often?

The penalty usually surprises people in a few common situations. None of these are unusual, and most are avoidable with a little planning:

  • People who feel healthy at 65. They take no regular medications, so they skip a drug plan. Years later a new prescription arrives and the months add up.
  • People leaving a job. Retiring and enrolling in Medicare can leave a gap between employer coverage ending and Part D starting. Counting days matters.
  • People who assumed other coverage was enough. Some coverage is not creditable. Always ask for the written creditable-coverage notice rather than assuming.
  • People who miss the Initial Enrollment Period. Your Part D clock starts when you are first eligible, which is tied to your Medicare Part A or Part B eligibility, not to when you decide to shop for a plan.

Because months are counted in whole numbers, even a few months can leave a small but permanent charge. Waiting "just one more year" to see whether you need a plan rarely saves money once the penalty is factored in.

A quick checklist before December 7

  • Find any creditable-coverage notices from past employers or unions.
  • Confirm you currently have drug coverage, through a standalone plan or an Advantage plan with drugs included.
  • Look at your plan's Annual Notice of Change, which shows next year's premium and drug list. Our ANOC guide explains how to read it.
  • If you believe you qualify for Extra Help, ask now. It can remove the penalty.
  • Remember that changes made during Open Enrollment must be received by the plan by December 7 and take effect January 1.

Already have a penalty? What you can do

You cannot simply switch plans to erase it, but you have options. Compare plans during Open Enrollment, since the penalty is added to whichever plan you choose and premiums differ. If you think the penalty is wrong, you can ask for a review of the decision; the letter from your plan explains how. Common questions

Part D Late Penalty FAQ

Does the Part D late enrollment penalty go away?
Generally no. Medicare.gov says the penalty usually lasts as long as you have Medicare drug coverage, even if you switch plans. If you receive Extra Help, you do not pay it.
What counts as creditable drug coverage?
Drug coverage from sources such as an employer or union plan that is expected to pay, on average, at least as much as Medicare's standard Part D coverage. Your plan should send a notice each year saying whether its coverage is creditable. Keep that notice.
Can I dispute a Part D late enrollment penalty?
Yes. If you believe you had creditable coverage or the penalty is wrong, your Part D plan sends a decision letter with instructions to request reconsideration through an independent review organization. Gather proof of your prior coverage first.
