Lost your job coverage? Your health insurance options

Losing insurance through work is stressful, but you have more paths back to coverage than most people realize — and a strict 60-day clock to pick one.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · September 2026

Whether it was a layoff, a reduction in hours, or your employer dropping health benefits altogether, losing job-based insurance triggers something useful: a Special Enrollment Period that opens the door to new coverage outside the normal enrollment calendar. The hard part isn't finding an option — it's figuring out which of the four realistic paths actually fits your budget and your timeline.

Your four main options after losing job coverage

  • COBRA continuation coverage. Keep your exact same employer plan — same doctors, same network — but you now pay the full premium yourself, including the share your employer used to cover, plus up to a 2% administrative fee.
  • An ACA Marketplace plan. Shop Marketplace plans from multiple carriers. Depending on your household income, you may qualify for a premium tax credit that brings your monthly cost down significantly — sometimes to a fraction of COBRA's price.
  • A spouse's or partner's employer plan. Losing your own coverage is a qualifying life event for their plan too, even outside their employer's normal open enrollment window.
  • Medicaid. If your household income dropped along with your job, you may now fall under your state's Medicaid income limit. Coverage can start quickly and often costs little to nothing.
Don't assume you know the answer yet. The "right" option depends on your household income today, not your old salary. A lower income after a layoff can unlock a premium tax credit — or Medicaid eligibility — that wasn't available to you a month ago.

COBRA vs. Marketplace: which actually costs less?

COBRA's appeal is continuity — you keep the same doctors and prescription coverage without any network research. But because there's no subsidy attached to COBRA, you're paying the full, unsubsidized cost of what used to be a group plan, which is often the most expensive kind of coverage per person.

A Marketplace plan may mean a different network or plan design, but the premium tax credit is calculated off your current household income — and for many people who just lost a paycheck, that credit is substantial. The only way to know which is cheaper for your situation is to run the numbers side by side, not compare sticker prices.

The 60-day clock, in plain terms

  • Your Special Enrollment Period is generally 60 days from the date your old coverage actually ends — not 60 days from your last day of work, and not 60 days from when you start looking.
  • Missing the window can mean waiting for the next Marketplace Open Enrollment period to get ACA coverage, so don't sit on this decision.
  • COBRA has its own separate 60-day election window, and electing it can sometimes be applied retroactively — worth understanding if you've had a claim in the gap.

Starting a new job? A few more things to check

If you've already lined up a new job, ask when your new employer's health benefits start — many have a waiting period of 30 to 90 days, which means you may still need a bridge option in the meantime. If your new employer offers an ICHRA instead of a traditional group plan, you'll be shopping the Marketplace yourself with a tax-free reimbursement toward the premium — worth understanding before you pick a plan. And if you're moving into self-employment or contract work instead of a new job, our self-employed health insurance guide covers the options built for that situation.

What if you only need coverage for a few weeks?

If your gap is short — say, a two- or four-week wait before a new employer's plan kicks in — it can be tempting to look at short-term medical plans as a cheap stopgap. Be careful here: short-term plans aren't required to cover pre-existing conditions, often exclude prescription drugs and preventive care, and don't count as qualifying coverage the way a Marketplace or COBRA plan does. For most people, a Marketplace plan priced with your updated income turns out to be a more complete option than it first appears, even for a short gap. If you're weighing a short-term plan, it's worth getting an actual side-by-side quote before deciding what you'd be giving up.

What to gather before you apply

  1. The exact date your old coverage ends (check your COBRA election notice or ask HR).
  2. An updated estimate of household income for the rest of the year.
  3. A list of your current doctors, specialists, and prescriptions.
  4. Your COBRA quote in writing, if you want to compare it against Marketplace pricing.
  5. Household size and any other coverage offers currently on the table (spouse's plan, new job's plan).

Common questions

Lost Job Coverage FAQ

How long do I have to enroll in new coverage after losing job-based insurance?
Losing job-based coverage triggers a Special Enrollment Period that generally gives you 60 days from the date coverage ends to enroll in a Marketplace plan, join a spouse's plan, or elect COBRA. Miss the window and you may be locked out of Marketplace enrollment until the next Open Enrollment period, so it pays to act early.
Is COBRA cheaper than an ACA Marketplace plan?
Usually not. COBRA lets you keep your exact employer plan, but you pay the full premium your employer used to help cover, plus an administrative fee, with no subsidy available. A Marketplace plan may have a different network, but premium tax credits can bring the monthly cost down substantially — often well below COBRA's sticker price.
What if my income dropped when I lost my job?
A lower household income can qualify you for a larger premium tax credit on a Marketplace plan, and in some cases for Medicaid instead, depending on your state and household size. It's worth re-checking your options with updated income figures rather than assuming your previous quote still applies.
