No-exam life insurance: how it works and who it's for

Skipping the needles and the nurse visit is appealing. But "no exam" covers several very different kinds of policies, and the differences matter.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · September 2026

Traditional life insurance often involves a paramedical exam with blood work, a urine sample, and measurements, then several weeks of waiting. No-exam life insurance skips that step. For many people it's a genuine convenience. For others, it's the only realistic path to coverage. But "no exam" isn't one product. It's a label that covers three quite different kinds of underwriting, and choosing the wrong one can mean paying more than you need to.

The three kinds of no-exam life insurance

1. Accelerated underwriting

Some insurers use data and algorithms to approve healthy applicants without an exam, often quickly. You answer a detailed health questionnaire, and the insurer checks records it's permitted to review. If you qualify, pricing can be similar to a fully underwritten policy. If the data raises questions, you may be asked to complete an exam after all.

  • Best for: Younger, healthier applicants who want solid coverage without the hassle.

2. Simplified issue

You answer a shorter set of yes-or-no health questions, and approval is based on those answers plus database checks. There's no exam, but there is underwriting: certain conditions can lead to a decline.

  • Best for: People with manageable health conditions who want coverage quickly and are comfortable paying somewhat more.
  • Trade-off: Coverage amounts are often capped lower, and pricing is usually higher than for a fully underwritten policy.

3. Guaranteed issue

No exam and no health questions. Within the eligible age range, you can't be turned down for health reasons. These are usually smaller whole life policies often used for final expenses.

  • Best for: People whose health would make other coverage unavailable.
  • Trade-off: Most use a graded death benefit, meaning the full benefit isn't paid for death from natural causes during an initial waiting period.
Don't start at guaranteed issue. Many people assume their health rules them out and go straight to the most limited option. It's often worth trying a simplified issue policy first. If you qualify, you may get immediate full coverage, a larger benefit, and a lower cost.

What you give up for skipping the exam

An exam gives the insurer more information, and more information can work in your favor. If you're in excellent health, a fully underwritten policy may earn you the best available rate class, which no-exam options can't always match. It can also allow much larger coverage amounts. The convenience of no-exam coverage is real, but so is the potential cost difference over decades of premiums.

Honesty on the application matters more, not less

With no exam, the insurer relies heavily on your answers. Life policies typically have a contestability period, often the first two years, during which the insurer can review the application if a claim is filed. Inaccurate answers can put a claim at risk. Answer every question fully and accurately, and ask if a question is unclear.

Find my best fit

Who no-exam coverage is a good fit for

  • People who need coverage in place quickly, for example for a loan requirement or a new mortgage.
  • Anyone who strongly dislikes needles or medical appointments.
  • People with health conditions that complicate traditional underwriting.
  • Older adults seeking a modest policy for final expenses.

If you need a large death benefit and you're in good health, it's worth comparing a fully underwritten term policy too. The exam may pay for itself.

No-exam coverage can also be a bridge. Some people put a simplified issue policy in place quickly, then apply for a larger fully underwritten policy afterward and decide which to keep once the new policy is approved. If you go this route, don't cancel any existing coverage until the replacement is actually in force.

What to have ready before you apply

No-exam applications move quickly, sometimes within a single session. A little preparation helps you answer accurately and avoid delays:

  1. A list of your current medications, with dosages and what each one is for.
  2. Any diagnoses, surgeries, or hospital stays in recent years, with approximate dates.
  3. Your doctors' names and contact information.
  4. Tobacco and nicotine use history, including occasional use.
  5. Your driver's license number and a sense of your recent driving record.
  6. How much coverage you need and for how long, so you aren't sizing the policy on the spot.

If you've been declined before, or you're unsure how a condition will be viewed, say so up front. An agent can often steer you toward carriers known to look more favorably on your situation, which can save you a decline on your record.

Common questions

No-Exam Life Insurance FAQ

Is no-exam life insurance more expensive?
Sometimes, but not always. Accelerated underwriting can offer healthy applicants pricing similar to a fully underwritten policy. Simplified issue and guaranteed issue policies generally cost more per dollar of coverage because the insurer knows less about your health, and they often have lower maximum coverage amounts.
How can an insurer approve me without a medical exam?
Instead of an exam, insurers typically rely on your answers to health questions plus data checks they're permitted to run with your authorization, such as prescription history, prior insurance application records, and driving records. Guaranteed issue policies skip health questions entirely and manage risk through lower benefit amounts and a waiting period.
What is a graded death benefit?
Many guaranteed issue policies pay the full death benefit only after a waiting period, often the first two or three years. If death from natural causes occurs during that period, the policy typically refunds premiums paid, sometimes with interest, instead of paying the full benefit. Accidental death is often covered in full from the start. Details vary by policy.
