How Does Cash Value Life Insurance for Kids Work, and Can It Help Pay for College?

Cash value can be tapped for any purpose, including tuition, but it grows slowly and accessing it has consequences.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

Some agents pitch whole life insurance on a child as a way to pay for college. Is that realistic? The idea is that the policy accumulates cash value that you can use for tuition, while also providing coverage. It can work for some families, but the details matter. Here is how it works and where it falls short.

Important: this article is educational, not personalized advice. Annuities and life insurance guarantees depend on the issuing company's claims-paying ability, can carry surrender charges and fees, and are not bank products or investments. Speak with a licensed professional and your tax advisor before moving money.

How cash value builds in a child's policy

A whole life policy on a child has a death benefit and a cash value account. A portion of each premium goes to insurance costs and fees, and the rest builds cash value, which grows at a guaranteed rate and may also earn dividends if the policy is from a mutual company. In the early years, cash value is usually small, then builds more noticeably over the long term.

Ways to access the money

  • Policy loans. Borrow against the cash value. Interest accrues, and unpaid loans reduce the death benefit.
  • Withdrawals. Take out part of the cash value, which can reduce the policy's benefits.
  • Surrender. Cancel the policy and take the surrender value, which ends the coverage.
  • Paid-up additions. Dividend-paying policies may use dividends to buy additional coverage, which increases cash value over time.

For details on loans, see policy loans and taxes.

Does it help with financial aid?

Many families ask whether life insurance cash value counts as an asset on financial aid forms. Under current federal methodology, cash value of life insurance is generally not reported as an asset on the FAFSA, but schools using their own forms may ask. Rules change, so confirm with the financial aid office. This is one reason some families consider the strategy.

Be realistic about the numbers. Because of early costs, a child's policy may not have enough cash value by age 18 to cover much of tuition unless funded aggressively, and aggressive funding has tax consequences.

Pros of using life insurance for college

  1. Flexible use. Cash value is not restricted to education.
  2. Guaranteed growth in whole life, though modest.
  3. Lifetime coverage that remains after college.
  4. Possible financial aid advantages under current rules.
  5. Control stays with the owner, typically the parent, instead of the child.

Cons to consider

  1. Slow early growth. Costs reduce early returns.
  2. Loans have consequences. Reduced death benefit and potential lapse risk.
  3. Commitment. Premiums are due for years.
  4. Better tools exist for pure education savings. For example, 529 plans offer tax-advantaged growth for qualified education expenses. See life insurance vs. 529.
  5. Overfunding rules. Funding a policy beyond certain limits can change its tax treatment.

A simple comparison approach

Ask for an illustration showing guaranteed and non-guaranteed cash value at ages 18 through 22, and the total premiums paid by then. Then compare to what the same money might do in other savings options, considering your tax situation and goals. The best choice may be a combination.

Who might consider it

Families who already have adequate coverage and savings, want permanent coverage for the child, value flexibility, and are willing to commit for the long term.

Alternatives for funding college

  • 529 college savings plans.
  • Custodial accounts.
  • Savings and investment accounts.
  • Scholarships, grants, and work-study.
  • Family contributions and payment plans.

A realistic expectation

Families sometimes imagine a policy that grows into a large tuition fund. In reality, the cash value of a typical child's policy after eighteen years may cover only part of the cost of college, and drawing on it reduces the lifetime coverage you hoped to keep. Think of the policy as a supplement and not the primary plan, and set your expectations using the guaranteed values, not the projected ones.

Coordinate with your overall plan

Before buying, make sure your own coverage is adequate, you are saving enough for retirement, and you have an emergency fund. College can be financed in many ways, including scholarships, work, and loans, but retirement cannot be financed with loans. Funding college at the expense of your own security is rarely wise.

Questions for the agent

Ask what the guaranteed cash value is at each birthday through age 22, how much of your premium goes to costs in the early years, whether dividends are guaranteed, and what a policy loan would cost in interest. Compare those answers with what a simple savings or education account could offer for the same money.

Common questions

Kids' Cash Value & College FAQ

Can I use my child's life insurance cash value for college?
Often yes, through policy loans or withdrawals, but this reduces the cash value and death benefit, and the amount available may be modest in the early years.
Is cash value reported on the FAFSA?
Generally the cash value of life insurance is not reported as an asset on the FAFSA, but rules and treatment can change, so confirm with the financial aid office.
Is it better than a 529 plan?
They have different purposes. A 529 is designed for education savings with specific tax benefits, while a life policy offers insurance and flexibility with higher costs and slower growth.
