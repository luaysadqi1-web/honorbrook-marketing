Inherited IRA and Annuities: Spouse vs. Non-Spouse Rollover Rules

A surviving spouse who inherits an IRA has the most options, including rolling it into their own IRA. Other beneficiaries generally must follow payout rules such as the 10-year rule, and a mistaken transfer can end the favorable treatment. Before any money goes into an annuity, confirm what kind of beneficiary you are.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

Important: this article is educational, not personalized advice. Annuity guarantees depend on the issuing company's claims-paying ability, and annuities can carry surrender charges and fees. They are not bank products or investments. Speak with a licensed professional and your tax advisor before moving money.

What the IRS says your options depend on

The IRS says the rules depend on your relationship to the account owner, whether the owner died before or after their required beginning date (the first date they had to take required distributions), and, for deaths after 2019, on whether you are an eligible designated beneficiary (IRS: beneficiary).

  • Surviving spouse: the IRS lists keeping it as an inherited account, taking distributions over your own life expectancy, and rolling the account into your own IRA, among the options, depending on timing. Whether a spouse is the sole beneficiary is determined by September 30 of the year after death.
  • Eligible designated beneficiaries (a minor child of the owner, a disabled or chronically ill person, or someone not more than 10 years younger than the owner) can generally take distributions over a life-expectancy period.
  • Other individual beneficiaries generally follow the 10-year rule.
  • Any beneficiary of an IRA can generally take a lump sum at any time, and taxable distributions are included in income.

Non-spouse transfers

The IRS lists rolling the account into your own IRA as an option for a spouse, but not for other beneficiaries. For employer-plan money, such as a 403(b), the IRS says a non-spouse beneficiary can make a direct rollover only into an inherited IRA set up to receive the distribution, which stays an inherited account with the required distribution rules still applying (IRS Pub. 571).

The traps

  • Taking a check. A distribution paid to a non-spouse beneficiary is generally taxable income, not a rollover.
  • Losing the “inherited” label. The account must stay titled as an inherited account unless you are a spouse electing to treat it as your own.
  • A payout schedule that does not fit. If you have to take money out on a set schedule and the annuity charges for early withdrawals, ask for the surrender rules in writing before you buy.
  • Inherited Roth IRAs. The IRS says beneficiaries of Roth IRAs are generally subject to required distribution rules too.

Where an annuity fits

Some carriers offer annuities that can be held in an inherited IRA. Availability, payout choices and whether a contract can meet the required distribution schedule vary by carrier and state, so ask for the answer in writing before moving anything. Questions to ask

  • Am I the spouse, a minor child, disabled or chronically ill, or someone else?
  • When did the owner die, and had they already reached their required beginning date?
  • Will the transfer be trustee-to-trustee into an account titled as inherited?
  • Does the annuity’s payout and surrender schedule fit the required withdrawals?

Common questions

Inherited IRA FAQ

Can a non-spouse roll an inherited IRA into their own IRA?
The IRS lists rolling the account into your own IRA as an option for a spouse, not for other beneficiaries. A non-spouse generally moves inherited money only by direct transfer into an account that stays titled as inherited.
What is the 10-year rule?
Most individual beneficiaries who are not eligible designated beneficiaries generally have 10 years from the owner’s death to withdraw the full balance. The timing details depend on the facts, so confirm with a tax professional.
Can I buy an annuity with an inherited IRA?
Some carriers allow it, but availability varies. The annuity cannot change the required withdrawal timeline.
