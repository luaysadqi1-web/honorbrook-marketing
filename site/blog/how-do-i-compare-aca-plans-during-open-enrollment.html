How do I compare ACA plans during open enrollment? A checklist beyond the premium

Compare plans by your estimated total yearly cost, not just the monthly premium: add the premium for 12 months to the deductible, copays and coinsurance you expect to pay, then confirm your doctors and drugs are covered. Open Enrollment for 2027 runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027.

By Luay Sadqi, Licensed Agent (NPN 21370662) · October 2026

When the plan list loads, the monthly premium is the biggest number on the screen, so it is the easiest thing to sort by. It is also only one piece of what you will actually pay. This guide walks through the comparison in a sensible order, using the steps HealthCare.gov recommends.

Key dates for 2027 coverage, per HealthCare.gov
  • Nov 1, 2026: Open Enrollment begins.
  • Dec 15, 2026: Deadline to enroll or change plans for coverage that starts Jan 1, 2027.
  • Jan 15, 2027: Open Enrollment ends. Coverage for sign-ups from Dec 16 through Jan 15 starts Feb 1.

Step 1: Estimate your total yearly cost

HealthCare.gov advises comparing estimated total yearly costs, not just premiums. Multiply each plan's monthly premium by 12, then add what you expect to pay for care. Three terms matter:

  • Deductible: what you pay before the plan starts sharing costs for most services.
  • Copays and coinsurance: your share of costs after the deductible.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum: the most you pay for covered, in-network services in a year. After that, the plan pays 100% of covered services.

If you rarely see a doctor, a higher deductible may be fine. If you take regular medication or expect a procedure, a plan with a lower deductible can cost less overall even with a higher premium. Our deductible vs. out-of-pocket maximum guide has a worked example.

Step 2: Check your doctors, hospitals and drugs

A plan is only useful if the providers you use are in its network. HealthCare.gov explains that plan type affects this: an HMO generally will not cover out-of-network care except in an emergency, while a PPO lets you go outside the network for an additional cost. Our HMO vs. PPO vs. EPO guide covers the differences.

Check each plan's drug list, called a formulary, too. HealthCare.gov notes that plans place drugs in tiers with different cost sharing, and that pharmacy networks can differ. Search by your exact medications and your pharmacy.

Step 3: Understand the metal levels

Bronze, Silver, Gold and Platinum describe how costs are split, not the quality of care, per HealthCare.gov. Roughly, the plan pays about 60% of costs for Bronze, 70% for Silver, 80% for Gold and 90% for Platinum. Lower tiers tend to have lower premiums and higher costs when you get care. See Bronze vs. Silver vs. Gold for more.

Step 4: Check for savings you may qualify for

  • Premium tax credits can lower your monthly premium on any metal level if you qualify.
  • Cost-sharing reductions lower your deductible, copays and coinsurance, but only on Silver plans. If you may qualify, compare Silver plans first.

Eligibility depends on your income and household, and it is worth confirming how your estimated income is entered. Our guide on keeping your subsidy if your income changes covers what happens mid-year.

Step 5: If you are already enrolled, do not skip renewal

If your plan renews automatically, HealthCare.gov notes you can still pick a different plan by December 15 for a January 1 start, and change until January 15. Premiums, networks and drug lists can change from year to year, so review before accepting the default.

A quick comparison checklist

  • Premium × 12 plus expected deductible, copays and coinsurance.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum you could realistically reach.
  • Each doctor, hospital and pharmacy you use, confirmed in network.
  • Each prescription, confirmed on the drug list at an acceptable tier.
  • Metal level and any Silver-plan savings.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Sorting only by premium. The cheapest plan on the list can have the highest deductible.
  • Assuming last year's network still applies. Doctors and hospitals join and leave networks. Recheck every year.
  • Forgetting the pharmacy. A covered drug can still cost more at an out-of-network pharmacy.
  • Ignoring the out-of-pocket maximum. It is the number that tells you the worst case for a bad year.

Common questions

Comparing ACA Plans FAQ

Is the cheapest ACA plan the best one for me?
Not necessarily. HealthCare.gov advises comparing your estimated total yearly costs, not just the premium. A plan with a low premium can have a high deductible, so what you pay when you use care can outweigh the savings.
Can I change my plan after I enroll during Open Enrollment?
Per HealthCare.gov, if you enroll or change plans by December 15, coverage can start January 1, and you can still change plans until Open Enrollment ends on January 15. Outside that window you generally need a Special Enrollment Period.
Do I need to pick a Silver plan to get extra savings on deductibles?
If you qualify for cost-sharing reductions, HealthCare.gov says the savings apply only to Silver plans. Premium tax credits, by contrast, can be used with any metal level. Eligibility depends on your income and household, so confirm it when you apply.
