What is Medicare Part D?

Medicare Part D is prescription drug coverage offered through private insurance companies approved by Medicare. It helps pay for the medications you pick up at the pharmacy or receive by mail. You can get Part D as a standalone plan paired with Original Medicare (or a Medigap policy), or built into most Medicare Advantage plans.

The mistake that costs people the most: choosing a Part D plan by its monthly premium. A $0 or low-premium plan can end up far more expensive if it puts your specific drugs on a high tier or doesn't cover them at all. The only number that matters is your total annual cost for the medications you actually take — and that's what we calculate for you.

How Part D costs actually work

Every Part D plan has a few moving parts that together determine what you'll pay:

  • Premium — what you pay each month to have the plan.
  • Deductible — what you pay out of pocket before the plan starts sharing costs (some plans have $0).
  • Formulary & tiers — the plan's list of covered drugs, sorted into tiers that determine your copay. The same drug can be a cheap tier on one plan and an expensive tier on another.
  • Pharmacy network — using a preferred pharmacy can lower your copays.
  • Annual out-of-pocket cap — recent changes put a hard yearly limit on what you pay for covered prescriptions, which protects people with high drug costs.

Because these pieces interact differently for every person's medication list, the best plan is genuinely individual. The right plan for your neighbor may be the wrong plan for you.

Avoid the late enrollment penalty

If you go 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage after your Initial Enrollment Period, Medicare can add a late enrollment penalty to your premium — and it lasts for as long as you have Part D. The penalty grows the longer you wait. That's why it's usually worth enrolling on time even if you take few or no medications today. We'll help you avoid the penalty and pick the lowest-cost option that keeps you covered.

When can you enroll or switch Part D?

  • Initial Enrollment Period: the 7-month window around your 65th birthday.
  • Annual Enrollment Period: October 15 – December 7 — switch plans for the next year. This is the time to re-check, because plans change their formularies and prices every year.
  • Special Enrollment Periods: triggered by moving, losing coverage, qualifying for Extra Help, and other events.
Why an annual review pays off: Part D plans change their covered drugs and pricing every single year. The plan that was cheapest for you last year may not be this year. We run a free review each fall so you're never quietly overpaying.

Common questions

Part D FAQ

How do I find the cheapest Part D plan?
The cheapest plan isn't the one with the lowest premium — it's the one with the lowest total annual cost once your specific drugs, the deductible, and copays are included. We enter your exact medication list and compare every available plan to find your true lowest cost.
What happens if I don't sign up when first eligible?
If you go 63+ days without creditable drug coverage after your Initial Enrollment Period, Medicare can charge a late enrollment penalty added to your premium for as long as you have Part D. The penalty grows the longer you wait, so it's worth enrolling on time even if you take few medications.
What is the coverage gap or "donut hole"?
It's a phase where you may pay a larger share of drug costs after combined spending reaches a yearly threshold. Recent changes added a hard annual cap on out-of-pocket prescription spending, which protects people with high drug costs. We factor these phases into the total cost we show you.
Do I need a separate Part D plan with Medicare Advantage?
Usually no — most Medicare Advantage plans include drug coverage. You typically add a standalone Part D plan when you have Original Medicare or a Medigap policy. We'll make sure you're not paying for two drug plans or missing coverage.
