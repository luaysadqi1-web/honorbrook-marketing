What are ACA marketplace health plans?

ACA marketplace plans are individual and family health insurance plans created under the Affordable Care Act for people who don't get coverage through a large employer, Medicare, or Medicaid. They're sold through the Health Insurance Marketplace and by private carriers, and they're the standard way for under-65 Americans to buy their own coverage.

Every ACA plan must cover the same set of essential health benefits — doctor visits, hospital care, prescription drugs, preventive care, maternity, mental health, and more — and no plan can turn you down or charge you more for a pre-existing condition. Because the core coverage is standardized, the real differences between plans come down to monthly premium, the network of doctors and hospitals, the drug list, and how much you pay out of pocket when you actually use care.

Subsidies: how premium tax credits lower your cost

A premium tax credit is a subsidy that lowers what you pay each month for an ACA plan. Eligibility is based on your household income and household size — not your savings or assets — and the credit is applied directly to your premium so you feel the savings right away rather than waiting for tax time.

Many people qualify for substantial savings, and the income limits are higher than most folks assume. Households well into the middle-income range often still qualify for help, and some end up paying far less than the sticker price. There's no cost or obligation to find out: tell us your expected household income and family size, and we'll run the numbers for you for free so you see your real monthly cost before you commit to anything.

Don't assume you won't qualify: the income limits for ACA subsidies are higher than most people think. Plenty of self-employed people, families, and early retirees who figured they "make too much" turn out to qualify for a meaningful premium tax credit — it costs nothing to let us check.

Metal tiers explained

ACA plans are grouped into "metal tiers" that describe how you and the plan split costs. Higher tiers cost more each month but pay more when you use care. One important detail: Silver plans are the only tier that unlocks cost-sharing reductions — extra savings on deductibles and copays for eligible lower-income households — so a Silver plan can be the best value even when a Bronze plan looks cheaper on premium alone.

When can you enroll?

  • Open Enrollment: runs about November 1 – January 15 each year, though exact dates vary by state. This is the main window when anyone can enroll or change plans.
  • Special Enrollment Periods (SEP): triggered by a qualifying life event, these let you enroll outside Open Enrollment. Common triggers include:
    • Losing other coverage (job loss, aging off a parent's plan, losing Medicaid)
    • Getting married
    • Having or adopting a baby
    • Moving to a new area

Common questions

ACA Health Insurance FAQ

Do I qualify for a subsidy or premium tax credit?
Premium tax credits are based on your household income and household size, not your assets. Many people qualify for substantial savings — and the income limits are higher than most people expect. We run the numbers for you for free so you know exactly what you'd pay before you enroll.
What's the difference between Bronze, Silver, and Gold plans?
All three cover the same essential health benefits — the difference is how you split the cost. Bronze has the lowest premium but the highest out-of-pocket costs when you use care. Gold has a higher premium but pays more when you need care. Silver sits in the middle and is the only tier that unlocks cost-sharing reductions for eligible lower-income households.
When can I sign up?
Open Enrollment runs about November 1 – January 15, though exact dates vary by state. Outside that window you can still enroll if you have a qualifying life event — like losing other coverage, getting married, having a baby, or moving — which opens a Special Enrollment Period.
Can I get ACA coverage if I'm self-employed or between jobs?
Yes. ACA marketplace plans are made for exactly these situations — self-employed people, freelancers, early retirees, and anyone between jobs. Losing job-based coverage is itself a qualifying life event that opens a Special Enrollment Period, and you may qualify for a premium tax credit based on your expected income.
